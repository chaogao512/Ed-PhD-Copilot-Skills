# Discussion Patterns for EdTech Governance Papers

Use these paragraph patterns in order. Each paragraph has one function; do not merge functions.

## Pattern 1: Opening — What this study asked and found

- Function: reconnect the reader to the research question and give the headline answer in one or two sentences.
- Do: restate the question in slightly different words from the Introduction; state the direction and size of the main finding.
- Do not: repeat tables, numbers or participant counts from Results; do not begin with "In conclusion".

## Pattern 2: Finding-to-theory interpretation

- Function: explain what the findings mean through the theory anchors used in the study.
- Structure: finding → relevant theory concept → supported/refined/contradicted → what that implies for the mechanism.
- Example move: "The finding that school-level support, not platform usage, predicted teacher digital competence refinement is consistent with organizational change theory's emphasis on capacity building (anchor X), and extends it by showing that time allocation mediates the effect."
- Do not: cite a theory only to name-drop it; each theory mention must change the interpretation.

## Pattern 3: Comparison with prior literature

- Function: locate this study in the verified literature base.
- Structure: agree with study A on X; extend study B by showing Y; diverge from study C because of Z (with a methodological reason for the divergence, e.g., different population, design or outcome measure).
- Do: only use verified sources from `docs/verified-source-registry.md` or `CITE-READY` literature cards.
- Do not: manufacture a "research gap" that the study does not actually fill; do not stack citations without a comparison statement.

## Pattern 4: Unexpected and null findings

- Function: honestly explain results that do not match expectations.
- Structure: what was expected → what was found → three candidate explanations ranked by plausibility → which explanation the data can actually distinguish → what remains ambiguous.
- Do: treat null results as informative; check power and measurement sensitivity before interpreting a null as "no effect".
- Do not: silently drop unexpected results; do not invent post-hoc mechanisms presented as if they were hypothesized.

## Pattern 5: Limitations and weakened claims

- Function: declare what the study cannot claim. Use `limitation-taxonomy.md`.
- Structure: limitation → which claim it weakens → likely direction of bias (overestimate/underestimate/ambiguous) → what would be needed to fix it.
- Do: lead with the most consequential limitation, not the most comfortable one.
- Do not: list generic limitations ("small sample") without tying them to specific claims.

## Pattern 6: Implications

- Function: derive theoretical, practical and policy implications. Use `implication-mapping.md`.
- Structure: each implication must cite the finding that supports it and state the boundary conditions.
- Do: separate what is generalizable from what is context-bound.
- Do not: write implications that would apply identically to any EdTech study (e.g., "schools should strengthen training").

## Pattern 7: Closing and next steps

- Function: state the study's specific addition and the highest-priority next research step.
- Do: name the open question that follows most directly from the limitations.
- Do not: end with a slogan ("the future is digital") or a global policy call.

## Anti-patterns checklist

- [ ] The Discussion repeats Results.
- [ ] Implications exceed the evidence boundary (satisfaction data → effectiveness claims).
- [ ] Limitations are generic and not tied to claims.
- [ ] Theory is mentioned but does not do interpretive work.
- [ ] Unexpected findings are omitted or explained by invented mechanisms.
- [ ] Citations are decorative stacks without agreement/disagreement statements.
